A Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) is one of the most useful tools Canadians have for building retirement savings. Contributions can be deducted from your taxable income, and investments grow tax-deferred until you withdraw them. But the deduction only works within your **contribution room**, and many people are unsure what their number actually is.

This article explains how RRSP room is calculated, where to find your exact limit, and what to watch out for.

## How RRSP contribution room is calculated

Each year you earn new RRSP room equal to:

- **18% of your earned income from the previous year**, up to an annual dollar maximum set by the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA),
- **minus** any pension adjustment (PA) if you belong to a workplace pension or deferred profit sharing plan,
- **minus** any past service pension adjustment (PSPA),
- **plus** any pension adjustment reversal (PAR).

For the 2026 tax year, the CRA's annual RRSP dollar limit is **$33,810**. You would need 2025 earned income of roughly $188,000 or more to reach that maximum through the 18% calculation alone.

"Earned income" for RRSP purposes mainly includes employment income, net self-employment income and net rental income. Investment income such as interest, dividends and capital gains does not count.

## Unused room carries forward

If you don't use all of your room in a year, it isn't lost. Unused contribution room carries forward indefinitely, so someone who has not contributed for several years may have a significant amount available today.

You can also contribute now and choose to claim the deduction in a later year. That can make sense if you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in the future.

## Where to find your exact number

Your personal RRSP deduction limit is shown in two places:

1. **Your latest Notice of Assessment** from the CRA, in the RRSP section.
2. **CRA My Account** online, which is usually the most current.

Keep in mind that the limit shown reflects information the CRA had when it was calculated. Contributions you made early in the year, or a pension adjustment from your employer, may not be reflected yet.

## What happens if you contribute too much

The CRA allows a lifetime over-contribution buffer of **$2,000**. Excess contributions beyond that buffer are generally subject to a tax of **1% per month** until they are withdrawn or until new room absorbs them. If you have several RRSPs or a spousal RRSP, it is worth adding up all contributions to make sure you stay within your limit.

## The deadline for the 2026 tax year

Contributions made during 2026, plus contributions made in the first 60 days of 2027, can be deducted on your 2026 tax return. For the 2026 tax year, the deadline is **March 1, 2027**.

You can contribute to your own RRSP until December 31 of the year you turn 71. After that, the plan must be converted, usually to a Registered Retirement Income Fund (RRIF) or an annuity.

## Questions to ask before you contribute

The right RRSP strategy depends on more than how much room you have:

- How does your current tax bracket compare with what you expect in retirement?
- Would a Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) or First Home Savings Account (FHSA) fit your goals better right now?
- Would a spousal RRSP help balance retirement income between partners?
- How does your workplace pension interact with your personal savings?

Working through these questions with a financial planner can help you use your room in a way that fits your whole plan, not just this year's tax return.
