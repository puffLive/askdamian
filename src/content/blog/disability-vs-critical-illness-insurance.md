For most working Canadians, the ability to earn an income over the next 20 or 30 years is worth far more than any single asset they own. An illness or injury that stops you from working can put that income, and the plans built on it, at risk.

**Disability insurance** and **critical illness insurance** both help, but they solve different problems. Understanding the difference makes it easier to see where you might have a gap.

## Disability insurance: a replacement paycheque

Disability insurance pays a **monthly benefit** if you are unable to work because of illness or injury. It is designed to replace part of your income for as long as you qualify, which may be a few months or many years.

Key features to understand:

- **Definition of disability.** "Own occupation" coverage pays if you cannot do your own job. "Any occupation" coverage only pays if you cannot do any job you are reasonably suited for. Many group plans switch from the first to the second after two years.
- **Waiting period.** Benefits begin after a waiting period, commonly 30, 90 or 120 days.
- **Benefit period.** Coverage may last for a set number of years or until age 65.
- **Taxation.** Benefits from a group plan are generally taxable if your employer paid the premiums. Benefits from a policy you pay for personally are generally received tax-free.

## Critical illness insurance: a lump sum on diagnosis

Critical illness insurance pays a **one-time, lump-sum benefit** if you are diagnosed with a covered condition, such as many types of cancer, a heart attack or a stroke, and survive a short waiting period, often 30 days.

Key features to understand:

- **You choose how to use it.** The benefit can cover treatment costs, a spouse's time away from work, mortgage payments, home modifications or simply breathing room during recovery.
- **It pays even if you keep working.** The benefit is based on diagnosis, not on your ability to work.
- **Covered conditions vary.** Each policy defines which illnesses are covered and what qualifies. Read the definitions carefully.
- **Taxation.** For a personally owned policy, the benefit is generally received tax-free.

## Side by side

| | Disability insurance | Critical illness insurance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it pays | Monthly income | One lump sum |
| Triggered by | Being unable to work | Diagnosis of a covered condition |
| Main purpose | Replace lost income | Cover costs and give flexibility |
| Covers accidents and many illnesses | Yes | Only listed conditions |

## Government programs have limits

Public programs offer a safety net, but they are not designed to replace a full income. Employment Insurance sickness benefits are temporary and capped, and the Canada Pension Plan disability benefit has a strict definition of disability that many claimants do not meet. Group coverage through work can also end if you change jobs.

## Do you need one, the other or both?

The answer depends on your situation:

- Self-employed professionals and business owners often have no group coverage at all.
- Households that rely on one main income are especially exposed to disability.
- Families with a mortgage and young children may value the flexibility of a lump sum.
- Your health history, occupation and budget all affect what coverage is available and what it costs.

Premiums are generally lower and approval is easier when you are younger and healthy, so it pays to review your coverage before you need it. A financial planner can look at your existing group benefits, savings and obligations and help you decide how much protection makes sense for your family.
