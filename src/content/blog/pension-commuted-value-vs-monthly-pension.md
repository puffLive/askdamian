If you belong to a defined benefit (DB) pension plan and you leave your employer, change jobs or approach retirement, you may be offered a choice: keep a **monthly pension** for life, or take the **commuted value**, a lump sum that represents what your future pension is worth today.

It is one of the largest financial decisions many Canadians ever make, and it usually cannot be reversed. Here is how to think it through.

## What is a commuted value?

The commuted value is the present value of the pension you have earned. Your plan's actuaries calculate it using standard assumptions about interest rates and life expectancy.

Interest rates matter a great deal. When rates are higher, commuted values are generally lower, and when rates fall, commuted values tend to rise. Two people with identical pensions can receive very different lump sums depending on when they leave.

## How the commuted value is paid out

A commuted value is not simply deposited into your bank account:

- **The locked-in portion.** The Income Tax Act sets a maximum amount that can be transferred on a tax-deferred basis. That amount usually goes into a locked-in account, such as a Locked-In Retirement Account (LIRA). Locked-in funds can only be withdrawn under the rules set by pension legislation.
- **The taxable cash portion.** Any amount above that maximum is paid to you as cash and is taxable in the year you receive it. You may be able to reduce the tax by transferring some of it to your RRSP if you have unused contribution room.

The taxable portion can be large, so it is important to understand the numbers before you elect.

## The case for keeping the monthly pension

- **Income for life.** Payments continue no matter how long you live, which protects against outliving your savings.
- **No investment risk.** Market downturns are the plan's problem, not yours.
- **Possible indexation.** Some plans increase payments with inflation.
- **Survivor benefits.** A joint and survivor pension can continue income for your spouse.
- **Simplicity.** There is no portfolio to manage and fewer decisions to make.

## The case for taking the commuted value

- **Control and flexibility.** You decide how the money is invested and, within the locked-in rules, how you draw income.
- **Estate value.** Money remaining in your accounts can pass to your beneficiaries, while a pension usually ends at death or after any survivor or guarantee period.
- **Health and life expectancy.** If you have reason to expect a shorter-than-average lifespan, the lump sum may deliver more value.
- **Concerns about the plan.** If the plan is underfunded or the employer's future is uncertain, some people prefer to hold the assets themselves.

## Questions to answer before you decide

1. What is your plan's funded status? Some underfunded plans pay commuted values in instalments.
2. How much of the commuted value would be taxable cash, and how much RRSP room do you have?
3. Is the pension indexed to inflation, and are there survivor or bridge benefits?
4. Do retiree health or dental benefits depend on staying in the plan?
5. Are you comfortable managing investments, or paying someone to help?
6. How does this decision fit with your other income, such as CPP, OAS and personal savings?
7. When is the election deadline? Plans usually give you a limited window to choose.

## Getting a second opinion

Comparing these options means projecting income, taxes, investment returns and estate values under different scenarios. Because the decision is usually permanent, it is worth reviewing your plan's statement with an independent financial planner before you sign the election form.
