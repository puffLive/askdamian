The Registered Disability Savings Plan (RDSP) helps Canadians with disabilities, and their families, build long-term financial security. What makes it stand out is the government money that can be added to the plan through the **Canada Disability Savings Grant** and the **Canada Disability Savings Bond**.

## Who can open an RDSP?

In general, the beneficiary must:

- be eligible for the Disability Tax Credit (DTC),
- be a resident of Canada with a valid Social Insurance Number when the plan is opened, and
- be under age 60 when the plan is opened.

Parents, legal representatives and, in some cases, the beneficiary can open the plan. Anyone can contribute with the plan holder's written permission.

## The Canada Disability Savings Grant

The grant matches personal contributions. The matching rate depends on the beneficiary's family net income (the parents' income while the beneficiary is a minor):

| Family net income | Matching rate |
| --- | --- |
| At or below the annual threshold | 300% on the first $500 and 200% on the next $1,000 contributed |
| Above the annual threshold | 100% on the first $1,000 contributed |

The maximum grant is **$3,500 per year**, up to a lifetime limit of **$70,000**. The income threshold is adjusted each year, so confirm the current figure with Employment and Social Development Canada.

## The Canada Disability Savings Bond

The bond is designed for lower-income families and **does not require any contributions**. Eligible beneficiaries can receive up to **$1,000 per year**, to a lifetime limit of **$20,000**.

## Catching up on missed years

If an RDSP was opened late, unused grant and bond entitlements can be carried forward for up to 10 years. The plan can receive up to $10,500 in grants and $11,000 in bonds in a single year when catching up, which can make opening an RDSP worthwhile even later in life.

Grants and bonds can be paid into the plan until **December 31 of the year the beneficiary turns 49**. Personal contributions can continue until the end of the year the beneficiary turns 59, up to a lifetime contribution limit of $200,000.

## The 10-year rule

Government money is meant for long-term savings. If money is withdrawn from the plan, some or all of the grants and bonds paid into it in the previous 10 years may have to be repaid. This amount is often called the assistance holdback amount. Planning the timing of withdrawals helps avoid losing government contributions.

## How an RDSP affects other benefits

Contributions are not tax-deductible, but the income earned in the plan grows tax-deferred. When money is withdrawn, the grants, bonds and investment growth are taxable to the beneficiary, who often has a modest income; the original contributions come out tax-free. RDSP payments do not reduce federal income-tested benefits such as the GST/HST credit or Old Age Security, and most provinces and territories fully or largely exempt RDSP savings and withdrawals from their disability assistance programs. Rules vary by province, so check how your provincial program treats RDSP income.

## Making the most of the plan

A few practical points:

- Make sure the beneficiary's DTC application is approved and tax returns are filed each year, since grant and bond eligibility is based on them.
- Contributing at least $1,500 a year captures the full grant for families under the income threshold.
- Think about the timing of withdrawals in light of the 10-year rule.
- Coordinate the RDSP with the rest of the family's estate plan, including wills and any trusts.

An RDSP is a long-term commitment. A planner can help you map contributions, grants and future withdrawals so the plan supports your family for decades.
